How we got here

This started as the class's Miami-housing regression recipe. Getting from there to a useful wildlife finder took a few wrong turns, which are worth more than the final numbers.

147,878sightings used
252species (≥200 sightings each)
42%real species in model's top 10 (random: 4%)
10%exact top-1 guess (baseline: 3%)
  1. Picking data. The assignment was a neural network on a new dataset. I wanted current data, not a 15-year-old classic, and something I'd actually use. I use the Seek app, so: iNaturalist.
  2. Pulling it. iNaturalist's public API, Miami-Dade County, research-grade and wild only. Five years was 362,699 sightings, too big for a GitHub upload, so I used three: 238,475 sightings, about an hour of polite paging at one request per second.
  3. Cleaning. Kept species-level IDs with a location and exact time, converted to Miami local time, and reduced each sighting to latitude, longitude, week of year and hour. A cutoff of ≥50 sightings left 737 species; raising it to ≥200 left 252, a better fit for a small network.
  4. Training the class recipe. Target = species, inputs = place and time. The recipe's 64/32 network scored 9% against a 3% baseline (always guess the Peters's rock agama). A 256/128 network reached 10.1% and started to overfit. Colab's free GPU quota ran out along the way, so that run trained on a CPU.
  5. The rethink. 10% looked useless, but it measured the wrong thing. At any spot and hour dozens of species are seen, so naming the one exact species is mostly impossible from place and time alone. An app gives a list. Graded that way, the real species is in the top 10 guesses 42% of the time and in the top 20 57% of the time, about 10× better than chance.
  6. "Most likely" vs "near its own peak". A list of the most likely species is always the same common lizards and birds. What I actually wanted: species that are near their own best time at this place. The model's output is each species' share of sightings at a place and time (like eBird's frequency charts), so each species is compared against its own best time at that spot. A "location score" keeps out species that barely occur there.
  7. Fixing artifacts. The first version returned nothing (I compared against each species' single best square in the whole county). Then single noisy night hours inflated the peaks, so peaks became the 90th percentile instead of the maximum. Black vultures showed up at 3 AM, so now an hour only counts if that species really was seen then. A spot where a species is almost never recorded now gets an honest "rarely recorded here" instead of a confident-looking chart of noise.
  8. Sanity checks. At Larry & Penny Thompson Park on an early-March morning: cardinals, mockingbirds, blue jays, grebes, saw palmetto. Same spot at 10 PM: cane toads, Cuban tree frogs, geckos and snails. Turkey vultures come out as an October–March visitor; black vultures as year-round residents; Cuban brown snails as night animals that peak in the wet season.

Limits

These are photo sightings, not surveys: they show where people looked as much as where animals are. For plants, "best hour" just means when people photograph them. Areas are ~3 km squares, so a single pond or trail can't be told apart from its surroundings. Squares with fewer than 20 real sightings are marked low confidence.
